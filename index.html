What you are looking at

One analysis engine, described further down, and two visualisers built on the values it produces.

Sphere — the spectrum, now
A quarter of a million points scattered evenly over a sphere of radius 4. Nothing about the sphere is animated directly: every frame each point is displaced from its resting position by several audio-driven fields, and what you read as ripples, spikes and swelling is the sum of them. It has no memory — every frame is drawn from that frame's audio alone.
Flare — a point of light, stared at
A picture of what the eye does to a bright source rather than of the source itself: a blown-out core, a corona, faint interference rings, the horizontal bar from a squint, and a field of diffraction needles split into red, green and blue (or left white, with Colour split off). Bass swells the core, treble excites the fine crackle in the rays, and each onset flashes the whole thing brighter. The flash is instant and the fade is not — the glare lingers the way an after-image does. It is rendered as linear light at half resolution, then tonemapped and dithered at full resolution, which is why the fixed cost is low and the gradients do not band.

Sphere: what drives each visual axis

Radial expansion — the swell
Bass pushes every point outward along its own radius, by up to 3.0 units at full scale. This is the dominant motion and what sets the overall size of the cloud.
Radial kick — the ripple height
The onset envelope adds up to another 0.5 units of outward push, decaying in musical time rather than fixed seconds — it falls over roughly a third of a beat, so hits stay separate at 170 BPM and breathe at 70. This is the term you perceive as a ripple travelling outward on each hit.
Radial breath — the idle pulse
The beat grid adds a further 0.35 units, phase-locked to the detected tempo and scaled by how confident the tracker is. Its job is to keep the cloud moving in time through sustained passages that contain no transients at all.
Turbulence — the churn
Mid offsets each point by 3D simplex noise sampled independently per axis, up to 2.0 units. Because the noise field itself scrolls with time, this reads as the surface boiling rather than as a shape.
Spikes — the needles
Treble displaces along each point's surface normal by noise × 4.0 — the largest coefficient in the shader. Because it follows the normal and the noise is signed, it pulls some regions inward while it throws others out, which is what makes it look spiky rather than merely bigger.
Point size
Each point's base size is multiplied by 1 + treble×3 + beat×2 + pulse×0.8, then scaled by inverse depth. Size is deliberately driven by the fast terms, so hits flash rather than swell.
Colour
Cyan at rest, mixed toward red as bass rises and then toward violet as mid fills in. The resting tone is nudged by the spectral centroid — dark, bass-led material sinks toward deep blue, airy material lifts toward icy violet — leaving the bass and mid moves exactly as they were.
Camera shake
Driven by the larger of the onset envelope and 0.6× the beat pulse, scaled by the track's crest factor, with a little steady bass mixed in.

The signal chain

  1. FFT — 4096-point, read as float rather than byte data. The byte API clamps to a fixed dB window and would throw away exactly the quiet detail the calibration needs. The browser's own smoothing is disabled, because it blurs the transients that onset detection depends on.
  2. Bands — folded into seven perceptual bands defined in Hz, not bin indices: sub 20–60, bass 60–160, low-mid 160–400, mid 400–1.2k, high-mid 1.2–3.5k, presence 3.5–8k, air 8–16k. Bin numbers only mean anything for one sample rate, and system capture and mic capture routinely differ.
  3. Normalise — each band tracks its own floor and ceiling, then has an exponent bent until its average output lands at 0.5. This is what lets one sensitivity setting work across genres: the engine calibrates per band, continuously.
  4. Detail bands — a second, much finer log-spaced set of 72 bands from 30Hz to 16kHz, calibrated the same way, for the modes that draw the spectrum itself rather than a three-value fold of it. It is kept separate from the onset band set below because the two want opposite things: onset detection wants few wide bands, so one transient lifts most of them at once, and a picture of the spectrum wants as many as the transform supports.
  5. Chroma — the spectrum folded onto the twelve pitch classes, and from that a key estimate, correlating against the Krumhansl-Kessler profiles for all 24 keys. What a bin contributes is not its energy but its salience: how far it stands above its own spectral neighbourhood. Raw energy gives an almost flat vector for any real mix, because a snare or a cymbal spreads energy over every pitch class at once and there are two orders of magnitude more noise bins than tonal ones. This is what the Key readout comes from.
  6. Onsets — spectral flux over 28 log-spaced bands from 40Hz to 15kHz, summing the positive dB change across all of them. Watching low-end energy alone only ever finds kick drums; this fires on snares, plucked strings, piano attacks and consonants too. The threshold is the running mean plus 2.0 deviations over about two seconds, and a hit must also be 1.35× the mean — so it self-scales from a sparse arrangement to a wall of sound.
  7. Tempo — autocorrelation of that novelty signal over an 8-second window, searching 62–190 BPM, with a log-normal prior centred at 120 to resolve half- and double-time peaks. Four successive estimates must agree within 5% before a number is shown at all; until then the readout says Searching.
  8. Gain — the auto-sensitivity loop described below.
  9. Smoothing — attack times of 28ms bass, 40ms mid, 18ms treble. Release adapts to the music: once tempo is known it is 0.28 of a beat, otherwise it slides between 110ms and 380ms based on onset rate. Fast material wants short releases so hits stay separate; sustained material wants long ones so it breathes instead of flickering.
  10. Limit — a soft knee from 0.75 up to a ceiling of 1.10. The shader's displacement amounts were tuned against a signal that could not exceed 1, so letting gain push far past that would change how the visualiser looks, not just how reliably it reacts.

Auto sensitivity

A closed loop that holds the average of the three smoothed bands at 0.42. Two things about it are deliberate, and they explain most of its behaviour.

It measures the output, not the input. The attack/release smoothing is asymmetric, which biases the delivered value above the raw one; regulating the input would therefore overshoot consistently. Measuring what the shader actually receives makes the target mean exactly what it says.

It falls faster than it rises — 0.9 against 0.25 in gain fraction per second, clamped to a narrow 0.6–2.2 range. Every failure mode of a loop like this ends with it being too loud, so the one dangerous direction is made slow and bounded.

Gain is also only allowed to rise when four things hold at once: the gate is open, six seconds of audio have been analysed, the delivered level is above 0.12, and the loudness envelope is within 12dB of its own recent ceiling. When the bands read low it is usually not because the gain is wrong — it is silence, or a track change the per-band calibration has not caught up with. Both resolve on their own, and integrating through them just means the correct level arrives multiplied by whatever the loop wound up to meanwhile. That is the failure you would otherwise see as the visualiser going wild at the start of a quiet song. Gain is always free to come back down.

The silence gate

Anything more than 45dB below the loudest recent moment counts as silence. That is generous on purpose — 40dB between a hit and the gap after it is ordinary musical dynamics, and gating that would starve onset detection of exactly the quiet passages it needs to hear. There is also an absolute backstop at −105dB, because a purely relative gate cannot recognise true silence: a steady noise floor sits at its own ceiling and so always reads as "playing". Since the usual way to start is to grant capture and then go press play, without that backstop the engine would spend the gap calibrating itself to line noise.

The gate opens in 0.15s and closes over 1.2s. It is meant to detect that the music has stopped, not to follow its level — closing at the speed of the signal would ride the gaps between hits and amplitude-modulate everything downstream.

Reading the telemetry

Tempo / Conf
Detected BPM and the tracker's confidence in it. Below 25% no tempo is reported at all.
Onsets
Detected transients per second, smoothed. Around 3.5/s counts as fully percussive material.
Gate
Open, closing or shut — whether the engine believes something is playing.
Gain
What the auto loop has settled on, or your manual value.
Crest
How much headroom the track leaves above its own average. Near 0% is a compressed master; high is an acoustic recording with real transients.
Key
The key the chroma analysis has settled on, or Atonal when no key stands clear of the alternatives — which is the honest answer for percussion, noise and a good deal of electronic music. It is deliberately slow: the correlations are averaged over several seconds before a decision is taken, so a passing chord cannot flip the reading.
Frame
Render time and frame rate. If this drops in Sphere mode, lower the particle count — the value turns amber to say so. Flare has a fixed cost that does not depend on any control.
Bass / Mid / Treb
The three values actually handed to the shader this frame, after smoothing and the limiter.
Profile
The engine's own classification of the material, as texture / tone.
